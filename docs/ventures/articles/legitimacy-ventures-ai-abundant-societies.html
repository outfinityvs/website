Executive Summary

Generative AI makes production cheap and abundant. That shifts scarcity toward something harder to automate: credible distinction. We believe this points to a major economy-wide inefficiency, potentially measured in trillions over time: society spends enormous resources trying to separate real competence from performed competence, grounded claims from persuasive noise, serious work from synthetic filler, and trustworthy institutions from reputational theatre. AI will amplify the inefficiency because it lowers the cost of producing claims, credentials, narratives, applications, expert-looking reports, and cultural artefacts.

This article frames Legitimacy Ventures as one of Outfinity’s strongest investment hypotheses. The thesis is that new companies can be built around legitimacy assurance, legitimacy guidance, and the core technologies used by decentralized brands: independent but recognisable networks of reviewers, analysts, curators, experts, and institutions that work under shared reputation and verifiable procedures. The goal is to keep the best part of the internet, plural perspectives and distributed intelligence, without giving equal visibility to low-accountability noise, bad-faith actors, or uninformed amplification.

The moment resembles the early internet and the early adoption of computers: old workflows become cheaper or obsolete, but new service categories appear around the new medium. In the same way that search, hosting, payments, marketplaces, analytics, security, and cloud software emerged around digital abundance, AI-abundant societies may need ventures that help people, companies, agents, and institutions decide what is credible enough to trust. This can also create new work for people displaced by AI: domain experts, reviewers, interviewers, curators, auditors, editors, and analysts can move from producing more content to evaluating, contextualising, and legitimising the outputs that matter.

The venture space can be read through familiar analogies. One direction is a YouTube for verified AI-assisted literature, where literary legitimacy brands help readers find serious work rather than synthetic filler. Another is a GitHub plus Crossref for scientific claims, where papers, datasets, methods, models, and replication attempts become inspectable evidence graphs. A third is a Bloomberg Terminal for founder and expert credibility, serving investors, enterprises, recruiters, and media teams with structured credibility dossiers.

One of the strongest early markets may be recruitment. CVs, cover letters, references, and university credentials are noisy, easy to inflate, and often weakly connected to a specific role. A legitimacy venture could create role-specific legitimacy reports: structured dossiers that evaluate a candidate against the actual requirements of a post, using work samples, interviews, domain expert review, evidence trails, reputation signals, and AI-assisted analysis. If done well, interviews could move later in the process and focus more on HR fit, management chemistry, team dynamics, and final judgment rather than basic credibility filtering.

Other opportunities are quieter infrastructure businesses: APIs for legitimacy signals, signed report repositories, provenance and revocation services, evidence-room software, and launch tooling for independent review firms. Consumer-facing variants could look like an IMDb or Rotten Tomatoes for post-AI culture, while institutional versions could resemble Morningstar for AI-era organisations, products, and public systems.

The economic mechanism is not only automation. It is a combination of curated AI, human judgment, and costly signalling. A person, founder, institution, or creator paying to be examined is not automatically legitimate; payment becomes meaningful only when the review is scoped, independent, contestable, and willing to produce negative or limited conclusions. In that form, spending money on review becomes a signal that the subject accepts the burden of justification, while AI helps reduce the cost of evidence gathering and humans remain responsible for final judgment.

This article is not a prediction that the market must emerge. It is a disciplined investment hypothesis grounded in several years of research on trust, reputation, decentralized brands, self-sovereign data governance, and distributed private data systems [23]–[26]. In this framing, social technologies are the human and institutional mechanisms that make judgment credible: brands, norms, reviewer selection, reputation, incentives, conflict-of-interest rules, appeals, membership, and public accountability. Technological infrastructure is the substrate that makes those mechanisms scalable and inspectable: signed reports, provenance records, verifiable credentials, permissioned evidence rooms, revocation, APIs, and machine-readable attestations. The core question is whether AI-abundant societies will pay for systems that make legitimacy harder to fake, easier to inspect, and useful enough for humans, institutions, markets, and personal AI agents.

1. From Production Abundance to Legitimacy Scarcity

The most important effect of generative AI is not only that machines can produce language, images, code, music, summaries, scientific hypotheses, strategic narratives, and institutional documents. The deeper effect is that output becomes detached from visible effort. A polished scientific abstract, literary fragment, legal memorandum, policy brief, grant proposal, pitch deck, cultural critique, or public statement no longer reliably signals deep labour, careful reasoning, domain expertise, genuine experience, or institutional responsibility.

This does not mean that AI-generated or AI-assisted work is necessarily low quality. The more precise claim is that AI weakens older signals of effort. An artefact may result from serious human-machine collaboration, but it may also be superficial synthesis, imitation, automation, manipulation, or noise. As production becomes cheaper, the social value shifts toward selection, provenance, interpretation, justification, and recognition.

The cultural emergence of the term “AI slop” captures this anxiety. Merriam-Webster selected “slop” as its 2025 Word of the Year, defining it as low-quality digital content produced in quantity by artificial intelligence [1]. The significance of the term is not that all AI content is slop. Rather, it indicates a public perception that generative systems can flood informational and cultural spaces with plausible but low-value outputs.

Trustworthy AI frameworks already address part of this problem. The European Commission’s Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI identify human agency, technical robustness, privacy and data governance, transparency, diversity and non-discrimination, societal well-being, and accountability as core requirements [2]. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework similarly characterises trustworthy AI through validity, reliability, safety, security, resilience, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness with harmful bias managed [3]. The EU AI Act adds a legal architecture around risk classification, documentation, transparency, data governance, human oversight, robustness, accuracy, and cybersecurity for high-risk AI systems [4]. The Council of Europe Framework Convention connects AI governance explicitly with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law [5].

These frameworks are necessary, but they do not exhaust the problem. A system may be transparent and still be used in an illegitimate context. A model may be technically robust but socially unacceptable. An institution may comply with formal rules but fail to justify its choices to affected people. A public figure may use AI efficiently while obscuring authorship, responsibility, or conflicts of interest. The concept of legitimacy names this broader problem.

The economic implication is straightforward but should be stated cautiously: if AI increases the supply of outputs, markets and institutions may increasingly demand credible mechanisms of distinction. The scarce layer is no longer only production. It is justified recognition.

2. Legitimacy as the Burden of Justification

Legitimacy should be understood philosophically before it is treated commercially. Political philosophy treats legitimacy as the justification of authority: the conditions under which power may be exercised and accepted as rightful [6]. Organisational theory similarly defines legitimacy as the perception that an entity’s actions are proper or appropriate within socially constructed systems of norms, values, and beliefs [7]. Procedural justice research shows that people’s acceptance of authority depends not only on outcomes but also on whether procedures are perceived as fair, respectful, neutral, and open to voice [8].

For AI-abundant societies, the most compact formulation is:

Legitimacy is power that has accepted the burden of justification.

Here, “power” should be understood broadly. It includes the power to publish, rank, recommend, classify, fund, hire, exclude, diagnose, certify, moderate, promote, represent, or define cultural and economic value. A platform has visibility power. A journal has epistemic power. A university has credentialing power. A museum has cultural power. A venture capitalist has allocation power. A regulator has legal power. An AI system used in hiring, welfare, credit, healthcare, or education exercises classificatory power through the institution that deploys it.

AI intensifies legitimacy problems because it can simulate the appearance of justification. It can generate fluent explanations, institutional language, legal style, scientific tone, ethical vocabulary, and persuasive narratives. But the appearance of justification is not justification. Real legitimacy requires visible analytical effort, technical competence, value clarity, bias awareness, accountability, and openness to criticism.

This distinction is central for any future business model. A service that merely improves appearance is reputation management. A service that makes the burden of justification visible may become part of a serious legitimacy infrastructure. The difference is not rhetorical. It determines whether the field becomes legitimacy washing or a credible market for disciplined judgment.

Legitimacy has several irreducible dimensions. Provenance asks where a result came from. Epistemic clarity asks what evidence and methods support it. Technical quality asks whether the system, claim, or artefact works under relevant conditions. Axiological clarity asks which values are embedded in the process. Bias clarity asks whose credibility is amplified or diminished. Procedural clarity asks how the result was reviewed and whether affected parties had voice. Accountability asks who is responsible. Contestability asks whether the result can be challenged. Social recognition asks whether relevant communities accept the process as appropriate.

These dimensions should not be collapsed into a universal score. Legitimacy is not a number. It is a structured, context-sensitive judgment.

3. Why Legitimacy Work Will Be AI-Assisted but Not AI-Owned

Legitimacy work is demanding because it requires reconstructing context. To assess whether a scientific article, literary work, institutional decision, AI product, public figure, or entrepreneurial claim is legitimate, one must inspect sources, data, methods, authorship, history, conflicts of interest, affected parties, technical evidence, institutional procedures, social impact, and alternative interpretations. At scale, this work cannot be performed manually.

AI will therefore be central to legitimacy work. It can compare document versions, detect unsupported claims, trace citations, summarise evidence, identify contradictions, map stakeholder positions, flag missing disclosures, detect patterns of bias, compare public statements over time, and generate structured drafts of reports. Existing documentation practices such as Model Cards and Datasheets for Datasets already show how structured reporting can make models and datasets more transparent by documenting intended use, evaluation conditions, data composition, collection processes, and limitations [9], [10].

However, AI cannot own legitimacy. In scientific publishing, the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors states that AI tools should not be listed as authors because they cannot take responsibility for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of work; humans remain responsible for AI-assisted submissions [11]. The principle generalises beyond publishing. AI can assist legitimacy analysis, but it cannot be the final bearer of responsibility. It cannot represent affected communities, answer morally for a decision, or assume institutional accountability.

The future division of labour is therefore hybrid. AI performs part of the analytical burden; humans, institutions, and communities perform judgment, recognition, and responsibility. This creates a new professional family: legitimacy curators, legitimacy analysts, legitimacy reviewers, legitimacy auditors, cultural validators, scientific claim reviewers, institutional interpreters, and AI-assisted credibility experts.

This is also relevant to the future of work. The IMF has estimated that AI may affect about 40% of global employment, with higher exposure in advanced economies [12]. The ILO’s 2025 work on generative AI and jobs similarly analyses occupational exposure through task-level automation and augmentation rather than simple replacement [13]. A plausible response is that some human labour will move from first-order production toward second-order judgment: curation, validation, interpretation, certification, criticism, and legitimation.

In other words, AI may reduce the value of producing yet another text, image, report, or pitch deck, while increasing the value of deciding which texts, images, reports, and claims deserve attention.

4. The Business Model: A Mixed Market for Legitimacy

A serious legitimacy industry cannot rely only on producer-side payment. If only the creator or institution pays, the service risks becoming reputation laundering. Yet it also cannot rely only on consumer subscriptions, because many legitimacy evaluations require access, documentation, interviews, data, and cooperation from the entity being assessed. The most realistic model is mixed.

On the supply side, creators and institutions may pay to obtain scoped legitimacy reports. These clients may include AI-assisted artists, writers, scientists, entrepreneurs, startups, corporations, universities, public agencies, foundations, investors, publishers, and public figures. Their motivation is not simply vanity. They may need to show that their work is not low-effort AI slop, that their AI use is transparent, that their claims are grounded, that their decisions are defensible, and that their authority can withstand scrutiny.

On the demand side, consumers, readers, viewers, citizens, investors, students, organisations, and eventually personal AI agents may pay for legitimacy guidance. The consumer does not want to inspect every dataset, verify every author, audit every claim, or evaluate every institution. The consumer wants orientation: which article deserves attention, which AI-assisted book is meaningful, which expert is credible, which company uses AI responsibly, which public report is independent, which founder makes grounded claims, which institution can be trusted in a specific context.

This demand is already visible in adjacent markets. Subscription-based creator platforms show that people are willing to pay directly for trusted voices, expertise, and interpretation; Substack announced more than three million paid subscriptions in 2024 [14]. Meanwhile, the Reuters Institute’s 2025 Digital News Report describes traditional news organisations as facing declining engagement, low trust, and stagnating digital subscriptions, while AI platforms and chatbots are emerging as new information channels [15]. These facts do not prove that legitimacy subscriptions will succeed, but they indicate a plausible demand for trusted orientation under conditions of informational abundance.

A mixed legitimacy market could therefore include several revenue streams:

Revenue source Function
Creator-side reports Creators, founders, scientists, companies, institutions, or public figures pay for scoped legitimacy assessments of specific outputs, claims, processes, or profiles.
Consumer subscriptions Individuals or communities pay for guidance about what content, experts, institutions, AI outputs, companies, or decisions deserve attention and trust.
Agent-facing APIs Personal AI agents, enterprise agents, platforms, and institutional systems access structured legitimacy signals, provenance data, and report summaries.
Institutional memberships Universities, publishers, professional bodies, public agencies, corporations, and cultural institutions subscribe to ongoing legitimacy monitoring and reporting.
Marketplace integrations Platforms integrate legitimacy signals into publishing, hiring, research, investment, cultural discovery, or AI-content marketplaces.
Public-interest funding Foundations, civic coalitions, or public bodies fund evaluations where the affected public cannot pay but the social value of assessment is high.

The mixed model matters ethically. If only producers pay, providers may flatter clients. If only consumers pay, providers may lack access and drift toward commentary. If both sides pay relatively small amounts, a more balanced structure becomes possible. The producer pays to be examined; the audience pays for orientation. The tension between recognition and discrimination can become a partial safeguard.

This model also opens a pathway toward personal AI agents. As agents increasingly represent users in search, purchasing, learning, investing, reading, and professional decisions, they will require external legitimacy signals. A personal agent may ask: is this report credible, is this author reliable, is this paper legitimate enough to cite, is this institution trustworthy in this domain, is this product claim supported? Legitimacy providers may therefore sell not only human-readable reports but also machine-readable attestations, feeds, and APIs.

5. Decentralized Brands as a Commercial Form for Plural Judgment

The concept of a decentralized brand addresses a structural difficulty. Legitimacy requires plural judgment, but markets require recognisable signals. A fully centralised legitimacy authority risks bias, capture, and monopoly. A purely decentralised ecosystem risks noise, fragmentation, and weak accountability. A decentralized brand offers an intermediate form.

The model tries to take what is best from the internet: plurality, independent voices, edge intelligence, fast discovery, open critique, and the possibility that valuable judgment can emerge outside a single institution. But it rejects the naive idea that every voice deserves the same distribution, attention, or institutional weight. A decentralized brand is not an unfiltered public square and not a permissionless megaphone. It is a selective social technology: it allows multiple independent centres of judgment to coexist, but asks each of them to carry method, reputation, scope, accountability, and the cost of being wrong.

A decentralized brand is a consortium-like organisation whose members work independently but share a common reputation, methodological layer, tooling environment, and public identity. The brand gives recognisability. The members provide diversity. The shared reputation disciplines the network.

This structure fits the nature of legitimacy because legitimacy is rarely monocentric. A scientific claim may require methodological, statistical, ethical, and domain evaluation. A literary work may require aesthetic, cultural, authorship, and disclosure assessment. A public decision may require legal, procedural, technical, and social-impact analysis. A founder may require technical credibility, track record review, funding transparency, and conflict-of-interest disclosure. A public intellectual may be credible in one domain and unreliable in another.

A decentralized brand does not eliminate commercial conflict. It changes the incentive structure. One member may face client pressure, but the brand as a whole has a long-term interest in preserving credibility. A weak report may satisfy one client in the short term, but it damages the shared reputation of the network. The brand therefore has an incentive to tolerate negative conclusions, publish scope limitations, preserve dissent, remove captured members, and maintain methodological seriousness.

The relevant governance concept is closer to polycentricity than simple decentralisation. Ostrom used “polycentric” to describe systems with multiple formally independent centres of decision-making that may interact, coordinate, compete, and mutually adjust [16]. Applied to legitimacy, this means that many actors can produce overlapping and sometimes competing judgments: technical auditors, ethical reviewers, domain experts, cultural critics, affected communities, civil-society organisations, professional associations, public regulators, decentralised networks, and AI-supported evaluators.

Digital infrastructure may help. W3C Decentralized Identifiers support verifiable, decentralised digital identity [17], while W3C Verifiable Credentials provide a standard way to express tamper-resistant, machine-verifiable claims among issuers, holders, and verifiers [18]. The European Digital Identity framework also points toward wider use of digital wallets and user-controlled identity attributes in Europe [19]. These technologies can support credential verification, report signatures, provenance, timestamps, review histories, and machine-readable attestations. But they do not create legitimacy by themselves. They support memory and verification; they do not replace judgment.

The decentralized brand therefore has three layers: human judgment, AI-assisted analysis, and verifiable infrastructure. The human layer gives interpretation and responsibility. The AI layer gives scale and analytical depth. The infrastructure layer gives persistence, provenance, and interoperability.

This makes the decentralized brand closer to an accountable network of specialised judges than to a social network. A social network often maximises engagement and treats attention as the scarce asset. A decentralized brand should maximise credible distinction and treat reputation as the scarce asset. Its commercial value comes from the fact that not everyone can speak under the brand, not every report receives the same status, and not every contribution survives review.

6. Independent Ventures Around Decentralized Brand Infrastructure

For Outfinity, the most attractive opportunity is not necessarily to operate every legitimacy brand directly. A more scalable thesis is to grow independent ventures that build the core technologies used by decentralized brands: the tooling, protocols, review systems, evidence layers, report formats, APIs, and governance infrastructure that let distributed firms work independently while still preserving trust under a shared reputation. In this model, each decentralized brand may specialise in a field such as literature, science, investment diligence, culture, education, or institutional AI, while the underlying technology ventures provide the common rails that make their work credible.

These ventures should remain independent because trust is domain-specific. A literary legitimacy brand should not be controlled by the same actors who certify scientific claims. A scientific review network should not be captured by a single publisher, funder, or platform. A founder credibility dossier provider should not become an advertising agency for founders. Independence allows each firm to carry its own reputation, methods, membership rules, and conflicts-of-interest policy, while shared infrastructure makes the results interoperable and machine-readable.

For investors, the opportunity can be read through familiar platform analogies. The point is not to copy these companies, but to recognise the kind of market position a legitimacy infrastructure venture could occupy if decentralized brands become a new trust layer.

The common pattern is that decentralized brands are the trust-bearing organisations, while core technology ventures are the infrastructure layer. A literary review brand, a scientific assurance brand, a recruitment legitimacy brand, an investor-facing credibility brand, and a civic AI legitimacy brand may all need report signing, provenance tracking, reviewer governance, evidence rooms, agent-facing APIs, and conflict-of-interest tooling. These shared components can become ventures in their own right. Their commercial role is to make independent judgment easier to perform, easier to verify, and harder to fake.

7. Markets of Application

The first market is culture. AI-assisted writers, artists, filmmakers, musicians, game designers, educators, and media creators may need ways to show that their work is not merely automated filler. Legitimacy reports could document AI use, human editorial responsibility, originality claims, cultural references, training-data concerns where relevant, and the declared aesthetic frame of the work. Consumers may subscribe to trusted cultural legitimacy brands that help them discover high-quality AI-assisted works rather than synthetic noise.

The second market is science. Researchers, journals, laboratories, universities, and funders may need stronger mechanisms for AI-use disclosure, reproducibility support, data provenance, methodological review, claim verification, and authorship accountability. The ICMJE position on AI authorship reflects a broader principle: AI may assist, but humans remain responsible for scientific integrity [11]. Legitimacy reports would not replace peer review, but they could complement it by making AI use, evidence quality, and uncertainty more visible.

The third market is entrepreneurship. AI can generate pitch decks, market analyses, product narratives, synthetic traction stories, and persuasive strategic plans. Investors may need tools to distinguish grounded founders from AI-enhanced storytelling. Founders may pay for legitimacy reports to demonstrate technical seriousness, claim discipline, ethical awareness, and credibility. Investors may subscribe to legitimacy feeds that help triage opportunities.

The fourth market is corporate and institutional AI. Companies and public institutions using AI in hiring, customer service, pricing, credit, education, healthcare, welfare, public administration, or internal governance need more than compliance. ISO/IEC 42001 already formalises AI management systems as a way to manage risks and opportunities associated with AI, including governance, transparency, and accountability [20]. Legitimacy services can sit beside legal compliance by translating technical and institutional evidence into credible public justification.

The fifth market is elite legitimacy. Cultural, scientific, technological, bureaucratic, entrepreneurial, and political elites will increasingly be challenged by AI-mediated scrutiny. Their status will not be accepted merely because of title, visibility, or institutional affiliation. They may need to show track records, conflicts of interest, real contributions, AI-use transparency, funding sources, domain limits, and responsiveness to correction. The goal should not be a social-credit system. The goal is to create better evidence around public authority claims.

The sixth market is recruitment and professional credibility. Hiring is already overloaded by inflated CVs, weak references, generic diplomas, noisy applicant funnels, and interviews that are often improvised rather than scientifically structured. A legitimacy venture could produce role-specific candidate reports that compress evidence before the interview stage. The report would not replace HR or management judgment; it would reduce the waste before that judgment happens. Recruiters and employers could spend fewer hours on basic screening and more on team fit, motivation, leadership chemistry, compensation, and final decision quality. Candidates with real but non-traditional ability could also benefit, because their evidence would not depend only on university names, polished resumes, or subjective first impressions.

This recruitment market could also enable small expert-led credential firms. Experienced practitioners in pharma, AI governance, cybersecurity, scientific computing, product management, regulatory validation, or enterprise AI could form decentralized brands that evaluate specific competencies and issue scoped credentials. These credentials would not pretend to replace universities everywhere. They could replace part of what universities cannot provide well in fast-moving markets: current, role-specific, industry-grounded evidence of capability.

The seventh market is consumer orientation. This may become the largest over time. People cannot personally evaluate every article, book, video, expert, product, startup, institution, or policy claim. As the internet becomes more synthetic, consumers may need trusted guides. Some will trust individual critics. Some will trust institutions. Some will trust communities. Some will trust decentralized brands. Some will configure personal AI agents to consult legitimacy feeds before recommending content, experts, products, investments, or institutional interactions.

8. Ethical Constraints: Against Legitimacy Washing

A legitimacy market is dangerous because it sells something close to moral and epistemic authority. The temptation to fake it will be structural, not accidental. Bad actors will want flattering reports, vague certifications, decorative badges, selective transparency, AI-generated explanations, and reputational laundering.

The field becomes credible only if it understands that its task is not to grant legitimacy but to make legitimacy harder to fake. A serious provider must be willing to issue negative, limited, ambiguous, or domain-specific reports. It must distinguish between “legitimate in this context” and “legitimate in general.” It must disclose who paid, who reviewed, what evidence was available, what was not inspected, what criteria were used, and what remains contestable.

Bias is central. Bias is not only statistical error. It is also the unequal distribution of credibility. Epistemic injustice theory shows that people can be wronged in their capacity as knowers when prejudice gives them an unfair credibility deficit or when dominant interpretive frameworks fail to represent their experience [21]. AI-mediated legitimacy systems could reproduce this problem by amplifying already powerful actors, dominant languages, elite institutions, wealthy clients, and high-status cultural forms. Therefore, legitimacy brands must be judged not only by technical competence, but also by whom they make visible and whom they render invisible.

A decentralized brand mitigates this risk only partially. Its strength is plural judgment under shared reputational discipline. Its weakness is that it may still be captured by money, ideology, status, or network effects. For that reason, the brand’s long-term credibility depends on negative capability: the ability to disappoint paying clients, expose uncertainty, publish dissent, recognise limits, and allow counter-evaluation.

The ethical formula is:

A legitimacy business is legitimate only if it increases the cost of faking legitimacy more than it increases the marketability of appearing legitimate.

9. Investment Thesis: Credible Distinction as Infrastructure

The investment case can be stated carefully. Generative AI increases supply. Increased supply creates noise. Noise creates demand for filters. But ordinary filters are not enough, because the problem is not only relevance; it is legitimacy. People do not merely want personalised content. They want to know what deserves trust, attention, money, citation, status, institutional use, or public acceptance.

For Outfinity, this is not presented as a prediction that must happen. It is an investment hypothesis: if AI lowers the cost of producing plausible outputs, then the market may shift value toward systems that make credible distinction cheaper, more visible, more plural, and more defensible. This hypothesis connects to prior work on decentralized brands in social media and beyond, decentralized brands in sustainable smart-city contexts, blockchain systems for self-sovereign data governance, and private data systems based on executable choreographies [23]–[26].

This creates an investable category between AI governance, creator economy, expert networks, certification, cultural criticism, compliance, rating systems, reputation infrastructure, and consumer subscriptions. The strongest companies may not be those that sell one-off reports. They may be those that build legitimacy networks: brands with recurring relationships on both sides of the market.

Producer-side revenue gives access to artefacts, data, workflows, and decision-makers. Consumer-side revenue creates independence and demand discipline. Agent-facing APIs create scale. Institutional memberships create stability. Public-interest funding covers evaluations that markets undersupply. Together, these sources can form a realistic business architecture.

The strategic asset is not the AI model alone. Models may become commoditised. The strategic asset is trusted judgment under a recognisable brand. The moat is accumulated credibility.

This credibility is fragile. That fragility is not a weakness; it is the governance mechanism. A legitimacy brand is valuable precisely because it can be damaged by bad judgment. The brand’s need to preserve its own legitimacy becomes part of its discipline.

10. Conclusion: From Human Production to Human Legitimation

AI will produce more of everything: texts, images, arguments, code, reports, summaries, rankings, explanations, simulations, and strategic narratives. As production expands, societies will need new ways to determine what deserves recognition. This is the field of legitimacy work.

The strongest formulation remains:

Legitimacy is power that has accepted the burden of justification.

In AI-abundant societies, this burden will increasingly be shared among humans, AI tools, institutions, communities, and brands. AI will help perform the analytical labour. Humans will remain necessary for taste, judgment, value interpretation, responsibility, and contestation. Institutions will provide context and authority. Consumers and their agents will demand guidance. Decentralized brands may become one commercial form that connects these needs.

This is not only an ethical agenda. It is a plausible business opportunity. Creators may pay to distinguish serious AI-assisted work from slop. Institutions may pay to make AI-mediated decisions defensible. Entrepreneurs and elites may pay to make their claims inspectable. Consumers may pay for orientation. Agents may require machine-readable legitimacy signals. Investors may fund the infrastructure that makes credible distinction possible.

The field must be built carefully. If it becomes reputation laundering, it will fail morally and eventually commercially. If it makes judgment more visible, plural, rigorous, contestable, and useful, it may become one of the central post-AI industries.

This perspective can be situated, modestly, within the broader research context of ACHILLES, whose focus on lighter, clearer, safer, and more trustworthy AI naturally opens toward the next question: not only how to make AI systems trustworthy, but how to build social and technical infrastructures through which AI-mediated results, decisions, creators, institutions, and elites become legitimately acceptable [22].
