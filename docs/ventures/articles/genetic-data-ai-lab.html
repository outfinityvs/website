Challenge

Genomic data presents a unique computational challenge. A single human genome contains over three billion base pairs, and when combined with epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic layers from population-scale studies, the data volume and dimensionality exceed what conventional bioinformatics pipelines can process efficiently. The problem is not simply one of storage or raw compute. It is a problem of representation: how to encode extremely high-dimensional biological information in a form that preserves the correlations that matter for scientific discovery and clinical decision-making while remaining computationally tractable.

At the same time, genomic data is among the most sensitive personal information in existence. Unlike a password or a credit card number, a genome cannot be revoked. Traditional anonymisation methods and even standard differential privacy approaches break down when applied to genetic data because the correlations that span the entire genome are precisely the signals that research and medicine need to extract. A privacy guarantee that destroys genome-wide correlation patterns destroys the utility of the data. The lab exists to solve both problems simultaneously: how to process genomic data at scale while respecting the unique privacy requirements that genetic information demands.

Approach

The lab's core technical approach builds on hyperdimensional computing and vector symbolic architectures, a family of techniques that represent information in very high-dimensional vector spaces where computation can be performed through simple algebraic operations. HDC/VSA is particularly well-suited to genomic data because it handles high dimensionality natively, supports efficient similarity search and pattern recognition across massive datasets, and can encode complex structured relationships in a unified vector representation. Current research includes adapting HDC/VSA encoding schemes specifically for genomic sequence data, developing efficient hardware-accelerated implementations, and benchmarking performance against conventional bioinformatics pipelines on population-scale datasets.

On the privacy side, the lab is investigating anonymisation techniques that go beyond the limits of differential privacy for genetic contexts. Standard differential privacy adds calibrated noise to query results, which works well for aggregate statistics but can destroy the genome-wide correlations that clinical genomics depends on. The lab explores methods that combine secure multi-party computation with controlled data release policies, synthetic data generation using generative models trained under privacy constraints, and privacy-preserving record linkage that allows datasets to be combined across institutions without revealing individual identities. The goal is to develop a practical privacy framework that preserves analytical utility while providing meaningful protection for genetic data subjects.

The third pillar of the lab's work integrates modern data collection and processing systems driven by natural language AI agents. Managing genomic data workflows today requires coordination across sequencing facilities, bioinformatics pipelines, clinical databases, and research repositories, a process that remains heavily manual and error-prone. The lab is researching agentic systems that can orchestrate data ingestion, quality control, pipeline execution, and result curation through natural language interfaces, allowing researchers and clinicians to manage complex genomic workflows without deep bioinformatics expertise. These agents build on the broader Outfinity agentic infrastructure and can be integrated with AssistOS for enterprise deployment.

Venture

The lab builds on existing research results in both hyperdimensional computing for biological sequence analysis and privacy-preserving data management. These results have been validated on public genomic datasets and demonstrate clear performance advantages over conventional methods for specific classes of analysis. The near-term focus is on producing reusable software modules for genomic data encoding using HDC/VSA, privacy-preserving query systems that operate over federated genomic databases, and AI-agent interfaces for genomic workflow management. These components can be licensed to bioinformatics platforms, integrated into clinical genomics systems, and eventually packaged as a standalone product for research institutions and healthcare providers.

A central product direction is Omics Firewall, currently in the planning and prototyping phase. Omics Firewall is designed to govern the end-to-end processes behind sequencing data, from ingestion and validation through to controlled access and downstream analysis, ensuring that every transformation applied to genomic and multi-omics data is policy-aware, auditable, and reproducible. Beyond workflow governance, the same platform integrates advanced anonymisation techniques for genetic data, combining synthetic data generation, privacy-preserving record linkage, and controlled release policies to keep genome-wide correlations usable for research and clinical decision-making while preventing re-identification of individual subjects. Omics Firewall is the concrete vehicle through which the lab's privacy research reaches production, both as a managed platform for research institutions and healthcare providers and as embeddable modules for bioinformatics and clinical genomics systems.

The venture path progresses from applied research toward a commercial platform for privacy-preserving genomic data management. Early revenue comes through research grants, technology licensing to bioinformatics companies, and consulting engagements with healthcare organisations navigating the intersection of genomics, AI, and data protection regulation. As the technical base matures and regulatory frameworks for genomic AI become clearer, the lab can incorporate as an independent company offering a managed platform for genomic data processing, privacy-preserving analytics, and AI-agent-driven workflow automation.
